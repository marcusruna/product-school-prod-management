    For discovery-driven viewers who feel overwhelmed by a 15,000-title library, Spotlight is a curated cinema space that puts trusted, hand-picked films in front of them, so they spend less time choosing and more time watching. For StreamLine, it retains our most valuable subscribers and turns a library we already own into a reason to stay.
1 · Module 1Problem Hook & Value Proposition

Cold-read your own hook

  • Is the business risk high enough to justify a new initiative, a high-stakes threat, or a minor inconvenience?:
    Yes, it’s a high-stakes threat rather than a minor inconvenience. The risk isn’t that users are mildly annoyed; it’s that they’re leaving for competitors who offer the curated experience they want. Churn among long-term, high-value subscribers compounds over time: each one lost takes their future revenue with them, and the market is moving toward curation whether we act or not. Doing nothing for 12 months means falling behind a shift that’s already underway.
  • Is the moment of misery a systemic problem or just an edge case?:
    It looks systemic, though I haven’t validated it with data yet. The problem comes from the product itself: a 15,000-title library with no clear point of view affects anyone who opens the app without a specific title in mind, which describes most casual viewing. That said, I’m basing this on the brief and my own assumptions, so I’ll need user research and usage data to confirm how widespread it actually is.
  • Does the value proposition actually remove the obstacle you identified, or is it a generic feature?:
    It targets the obstacle directly. The obstacle is too much choice and too little trust in recommendations, and Spotlight responds by narrowing the choice to a small set of hand-picked films with a clear point of view. The risk is execution: if the curation feels like just another row of algorithmic picks, it becomes a generic feature. Its value depends on users trusting the curator’s taste, which is an assumption I still need to test.

Channels

1. Phase 1, in-app (Weeks 4–5, during the test): For treatment Wanderers, the Spotlight rail is the first row on Home, headed “Picked by our editors.” No outbound comms run, so the test measures the rail alone.

2. Phase 2, email (after Day 14, if we ship or iterate): One email at 6 pm local time to opted-in lapsed Wanderers. It shows three Spotlight titles plus “See all 10,” which deep-links into the rail. Half of the lapsed treatment users get it and half don’t, so the email’s own effect is measurable.

3. Phase 2, push (after Day 14): One push per week on refresh day to opted-in lapsed Wanderers in the email group, never on the same day as the email, deep-linking into the rail. The reachable count is pulled in Week 2; if it covers under 30% of lapsed users, push is dropped.

4. Held back for an L launch: paid social (customer-list match rates are partial, and it needs a privacy review) and earned media (it would alert Power Users). Both are the first levers to add if the metric moves.

Success Metrics

  • Metrics:
    • Primary: share of Wanderer sessions reaching 30+ minutes, treatment vs. holdout, at Day 14 (floor +2 pts, goal +4 pts).
    • Mechanism: median time from homepage load to Play.
    • Lagging confirmation: Month 1 retention vs. holdout at Day 30.
    • Guardrails: Power User sessions/week at or above 4.6×, Power User churn not above holdout, and in Phase 2, unsubscribe and push opt-out rates.
  • Bad signals to watch for (checked daily from Day 3; the primary metric isn’t read early):
    • High rail views, low plays. Rail play-start rate falls below half of Trending’s. First check whether viewers have already watched the picks, and test thumbnails and one-liners. If the picks themselves are the problem, Editorial changes them at the next weekly refresh. Placement stays fixed during the test.
    • High plays, short sessions. Over half of rail plays stop before 10 minutes, meaning the picks attract clicks but don’t hold viewers. Editorial revises its selection criteria.
  • Likely post-launch decision:
    The most likely result is a lift between the +2-point floor and the +4-point goal. That means we ship to all Wanderers and spend the next sprint on the picks themselves. The risk case is a lift under +2: Wanderers watch 61% Trending, so an editor-picked rail starts against their habits. In that case we iterate on selection before retesting, rather than moving the rail.
Module 6

Individual Insights

Where the work was hardest, the moment my thinking changed, and what I learned.

Friction points

The hardest part was learning to read data deeply enough to build a plan on it. Before this course, I saw data in two or three dimensions: a number, a trend, maybe a comparison. Now I see how many more layers it has, like which segment a metric comes from, what it’s correlated with versus what it causes, and how one figure can change the whole plan. For example, I first read Spotlight’s 22-point churn gap as proof the feature worked, before realizing it only showed correlation and needed a holdout test to confirm.

Aha moment

My aha moment came when I understood that product decisions mean balancing customer needs against business needs, and prioritizing between them. That changed how I see roadmapping. It’s not a list of features but a sequence of choices about what matters most right now and what comes next. With Spotlight, that meant solving the Wanderers’ choice overload while also targeting the long-tenured subscribers the business was at risk of losing.

Key learnings

My key learnings are the value of A/B testing and of writing a clear hypothesis before building anything. Together they pushed me to think strategically about roadmapping: delivering solutions customers need while proving value to stakeholders. For Spotlight, writing the hypothesis first forced me to define success (more 30+ minute sessions for Wanderers) before deciding what to build or how to launch it.
